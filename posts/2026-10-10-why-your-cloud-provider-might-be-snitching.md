# Keeping Up With Tech: Why Your Cloud Provider Might Be Snitching (And What to Do About It)

Look, the cloud was supposed to be this magical place where your data floats around safely, accessible from anywhere, and nobody's watching. Cute theory. Reality is more like a group of giant companies arguing over who gets to hold the keys while governments peer through the windows.

## The Big Blue Playbook

IBM has spent decades perfecting the art of selling to businesses instead of humans. Under CEO Arvind Krishna, that's the entire strategy: hybrid cloud, the watsonx platform for enterprise AI, consulting gigs, and those mainframes that still quietly run a lot of serious stuff. They're not chasing TikTok or your personal photo storage. They want corporate budgets, regulated industries, and large-scale AI deployments that come with compliance checkboxes.

Investors have noticed. While the hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Oracle) dump absurd amounts of money into AI infrastructure, people are starting to ask if they're actually making returns or just... circulating cash and calling it growth. Free cash flow is getting squeezed. There's talk of a "capex pickle." Cloud spending is still climbing (public cloud on track to top a trillion this year), but the market has gotten pickier about pure spending stories.

Earlier in 2026 there was the whole "SaaSpocalypse" panic—AI was going to kill traditional software. Stocks got hit, then recovered when earnings showed more resilience. Enterprise-focused names generally held up better than pure consumer plays. IBM itself has been mixed; the stock is off its highs, and analysts keep asking when all that enterprise AI spending actually shows up in their numbers.

## When Countries Get Annoyed

Europe is particularly done with the status quo. AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud control roughly 70% of the European cloud market. The issue isn't just market share—it's the US CLOUD Act. That law lets American authorities compel US providers to hand over data *even if the servers are in Frankfurt or Paris*. A "Swiss region" or "EU data boundary" from a US company doesn't fully fix that, because the parent company is still American.

France has been loud about it. The Netherlands passed motions to reduce reliance on US tech. The EU is working on a Cloud and AI Development Act that would create sovereignty tiers for sensitive government and defense work. Local European cloud companies call some of the US "sovereign" offerings "sovereignty washing." Defense ministries worry that going too hard too fast could break NATO systems that already run on the big three. It's a mess of competing priorities.

## Ranking the Privacy Options

If you're trying to minimize foreign government access, a fully Swiss-owned provider with data centers only in Switzerland currently looks like the cleanest option. Switzerland isn't in Five Eyes. Its revised data protection law is strong and has an EU adequacy decision. There's no Swiss equivalent of the CLOUD Act that lets foreign authorities demand data directly. Requests generally have to go through formal mutual legal assistance.

EU providers (think OVHcloud, Hetzner, Scaleway) are a solid second—they avoid the US CLOUD Act and sit under GDPR—but they still live inside Europe's own law-enforcement framework. US providers sit at the bottom of this particular ranking because of how the CLOUD Act is written.

No place is perfect. Any country can still compel its own companies through domestic courts. But the difference between "US authorities can reach in because the company is American" and "they have to ask Switzerland nicely through proper channels" is meaningful if that distinction matters to you.

## The Short Version

The cloud market is concentrated, the spending is enormous, regulators are paying attention, and Europe is trying to figure out how much dependence on US providers it wants to live with. IBM is playing the long enterprise game. The privacy-conscious are looking at Switzerland. And everyone is watching to see whether the AI infrastructure binge produces real returns or just more circular spending.

Keeping up with tech means occasionally noticing when the infrastructure underneath the hype has some very large strings attached.
